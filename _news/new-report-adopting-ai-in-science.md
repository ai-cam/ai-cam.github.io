As AI capabilities advance, research institutions are looking for new ways to translate technological possibilities into scientific progress. 

A new report from ai@cam and the Bennett School of Public Policy at the University of Cambridge examines how ten initiatives around the world are tackling the practical challenges of AI adoption in science. It highlights the people, services, and institutional arrangements needed to connect advances in AI with scientific progress, and the role that research institutions play in shaping the future of science with AI.

The report, *From access to adoption: case studies of AI adoption in science institutions*, draws on experiences from the UK, Europe, the US, and Africa.

The case studies illustrate different ways institutions are enabling AI adoption: from AIchemy’s flexible funding, to KU Leuven’s shared engineering services; from Data Science Africa building skills and training networks, to OpenBind generating experimental data for AI in drug discovery; and from the UK Centre for Ecology and Hydrology preparing decades of environmental data for AI analysis, to the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts developing the testing and verification needed to bring AI-enabled science into operational use.

These examples highlight the range of capabilities needed to move from access to adoption. They show how researchers experimenting with AI in science identify new applications, expose technical limitations, and generate insights that can shape future AI development.

“Through delivery of AI for science initiatives and on-the-ground support for researchers, institutions are identifying new research applications for AI and exploring where technical limits lie,” said Professor Neil Lawrence, DeepMind Professor of Machine Learning at the University of Cambridge. “This approach — learning and innovating through experimentation — is helping to shape the future development of AI.”

The report argues that these experiences have implications for the next phase of AI for science policy. Investment in compute, data, and models remains important, but research institutions also need the organisational innovations to put these to use. That means supporting engineering and data stewardship, creating opportunities for interdisciplinary research, and developing funding and career structures that allow progress at the interface of AI and the sciences.

“The UK has made substantial investments in AI capabilities; the next question is how those investments become scientific progress,” said Jessica Montgomery, Director of ai@cam. “That requires institutions to have the capacity to experiment safely with AI in science, learn from emerging practice, and adapt as the technology changes. It also means thinking how we fund the people and services that connect AI advances to research practice.”

The report also suggests that adoption initiatives can help inform thinking about the future of AI. The experience of researchers working with AI provides evidence of emerging scientific opportunities, recurring technical bottlenecks, and the capabilities that need to be developed next.

The report is published in partnership with the Bennett School of Public Policy.
